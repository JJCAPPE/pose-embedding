## How pose embeddings have already been studied

The earliest directly relevant work is **Pose Embeddings: A Deep Architecture for Learning to Match Human Poses** from 2015. That paper learned an embedding for human pose matching and retrieval using a triplet-based criterion, and it is still important because it establishes the core formulation that your project also needs: pose comparison as an embedding-and-nearest-neighbor problem rather than only a classification problem. It is image-based rather than skeleton-sequence-based, but conceptually it is one of the clearest predecessors of your proposed work. citeturn0academia3turn2academia2

A second important line is **view-invariant pose embedding** from 2D keypoints. Google’s **Pr-VIPE** paper learns a compact view-invariant probabilistic embedding from 2D joints without explicitly lifting to 3D, and the later occlusion-robust extension reports gains not only for cross-view pose retrieval but also for pose-sequence retrieval, action recognition, and video alignment. That is unusually close to your intended robustness angle because it explicitly treats 2D pose as an ambiguous, noisy projection of 3D structure and uses probabilistic embeddings and occlusion augmentation to cope with that uncertainty. The associated POEM repository is public, includes training and inference code, later added temporal support, and explicitly added individual random keypoint dropout and cross-view pose retrieval evaluation support. citeturn2academia1turn29academia0turn24view3

A third line is **metric learning for skeleton action recognition under nearest-neighbor evaluation**. **SL-DML** and **Skeleton-DML** are especially relevant because they formulate one-shot or few-shot skeleton-based action recognition as deep metric learning and nearest-neighbor search in embedding space. SL-DML uses triplet loss over signal-to-image representations and reports gains on NTU RGB+D 120 and other modalities, while Skeleton-DML specifically proposes a skeleton representation for deep metric learning and also reports nearest-neighbor gains on NTU RGB+D 120. These papers are not full retrieval benchmarks in the information-retrieval sense, but they are highly relevant evidence that sequence-level skeleton embeddings for ranking and assignment are viable, and they show that triplet-style metric learning has already been used successfully in this domain. citeturn38academia3turn27academia0turn38academia2

A fourth and more recent line is **general-purpose human motion representation learning**. **MotionBERT** is the clearest representative here: it pretrains a motion encoder to recover 3D motion from noisy partial 2D observations, uses a dual-stream spatio-temporal transformer, and then transfers the learned representation to tasks including 3D pose estimation and skeleton-based action recognition. This is not a retrieval paper, but it is directly useful for your project because it gives you a strong representation backbone already trained to tolerate exactly the sort of partial and noisy pose observations your proposed evaluation will introduce. **MotionCLIP** is adjacent rather than direct: it learns a latent motion space aligned to CLIP, which is useful if you later want semantic or cross-modal retrieval, but it is less direct than MotionBERT for a first supervised retrieval baseline. citeturn1academia0turn26view1turn1academia3turn36view0

The newest retrieval-heavy work is mostly **cross-modal motion retrieval** rather than label-conditioned pose retrieval. **Text-to-Motion Retrieval** introduces joint text-motion retrieval over KIT Motion-Language and HumanML3D using contrastive objectives and several motion encoders, including DG-STGCN and their Motion Transformer. Since then, the field has expanded into cross-dataset studies, tri-modal retrieval, four-modal retrieval, and increasingly fine-grained alignment of text, video, audio, and motion. This matters because it shows where the broader “motion embedding” community is currently investing effort: contrastive alignment and multimodal retrieval. For your project, that is useful context, but it also means your proposed **pose-only, retrieval-robustness, contextual-loss** direction remains relatively underexplored. citeturn2academia3turn35view0turn30academia1turn3academia3turn4academia2turn4academia0

## Datasets that matter for this project

For a **first supervised retrieval benchmark**, **NTU RGB+D 120** is the most practical choice. Its paper reports more than 114,000 video samples, 8 million frames, 106 subjects, and 120 action classes, with standard cross-subject and cross-setup protocols. That gives you what contextual metric learning needs: many classes, multiple examples per class, and evaluation settings where retrieval quality matters across people and views. It also matches the recommendation already written into your project brief. The weakness is that NTU actions are broad daily and interaction classes rather than subtle sports movement classes, so it is better as an engineering and methodology benchmark than as a final sport-specific demonstration. citeturn6academia0 fileciteturn0file0L538-L540 fileciteturn0file0L547-L553

For **clean motion representation learning**, **AMASS** remains one of the best resources. It unifies 15 MoCap datasets in a common representation, with more than 40 hours of motion data, over 300 subjects, and more than 11,000 motions. The main advantage is clean, physically plausible 3D motion with unified body parameterization; the main downside is that retrieval labels are not as clean and ready-made as NTU’s action labels unless you combine AMASS with **BABEL**, which adds sequence-level and frame-level action annotations over AMASS motions. BABEL contributes about 43 hours of mocap sequences, over 28,000 sequence labels, over 63,000 frame labels, and more than 250 action categories. This AMASS-plus-BABEL stack is excellent if your goal evolves from action retrieval into **general motion representation plus retrieval**. citeturn6academia1turn7academia1

For **sports relevance**, **SportsPose** is unusually valuable. Its paper reports more than 176,000 3D poses from 24 subjects across five sports activities and emphasizes that its movements are more dynamic than Human3.6M and 3DPW in distal joints such as wrists and ankles. That is exactly the sort of distribution shift that matters if your eventual application is sports movement analysis. The trade-off is scale and label granularity: it is far smaller and narrower than NTU, so it is better as a second-stage validation set than as the first baseline dataset. citeturn6academia2

For **controlled pose-estimation benchmarks**, **H3WB** and the broader Human3.6M ecosystem remain useful. H3WB adds 133 whole-body keypoints over 100,000 images on top of Human3.6M, which is helpful if you care about hands, face, and feet or want to study incomplete-keypoint robustness. But as a retrieval dataset it is weaker than NTU because it is fundamentally a pose-estimation benchmark rather than a motion-search benchmark. citeturn33academia0

For **fine-grained sports action structure**, **FineGym** is relevant. It provides temporally annotated gymnastics actions and sub-actions organized in a hierarchy, which is attractive if you later want retrieval of action phases or technique segments rather than whole-sequence class retrieval. The weakness is that FineGym is video-first, so unless you adopt a pose extraction pipeline, it is not as frictionless as NTU skeletons or AMASS motions. citeturn8academia0

For **cross-modal retrieval baselines**, the standard datasets are **KIT Motion-Language** and **HumanML3D**, often with BABEL in the background. KIT Motion-Language contains 3,911 motions, 11.23 hours, and 6,278 natural-language annotations. These are highly useful if you later want to evaluate whether a pose-only embedding also supports caption alignment or multimodal search, but they are not the right first benchmark for testing contextual loss against skeleton-class retrieval. citeturn7academia0turn2academia3turn30academia1

## Architectures, objectives, and evaluation practice

The architecture landscape is dominated by **GCN-based skeleton encoders, transformer-based spatio-temporal encoders, and heatmap-based 3D CNN variants**. **ST-GCN** established the graph-convolution formulation for skeleton sequences, **ST-TR** and related transformer models replaced graph operations with self-attention over joints and time, **MST-GCN** widened spatial and temporal receptive fields, and **PoseC3D** abandoned graph sequences in favor of 3D pose heatmap stacks. A survey of 3D skeleton action recognition organizes the field around RNN, CNN, GCN, and Transformer families, confirming that these are now the canonical architectural buckets. citeturn9academia0turn9academia1turn9academia2turn6academia3turn37academia0

For your particular project, two architectures stand out. **PoseC3D** is attractive because its own paper explicitly claims it is more robust to pose-estimation noise and cross-dataset transfer than GCN-based baselines. **MotionBERT** is attractive because it pretrains directly on noisy partial 2D observations and exposes an encoder that can be repurposed for downstream sequence embeddings. If your question is specifically “does contextual loss improve robustness under pose corruption,” both backbones give you a strong starting point, but they answer slightly different subquestions: PoseC3D is a robustness-minded supervised baseline; MotionBERT is a representation-rich pretrained backbone. citeturn6academia3turn1academia0turn26view1

On the loss side, the relevant baseline family is still the classical deep metric-learning stack: **contrastive loss, triplet loss, supervised contrastive loss, classification proxies, and ranking-based objectives such as Smooth-AP**. Two facts matter here. First, **A Metric Learning Reality Check** argues that many claimed advances in deep metric learning are smaller than papers suggest, which means your baseline selection needs to be careful and strong, not token. Second, Brian Kulis’s contextual-loss paper is not competing against a weak baseline set; it explicitly positions itself against contrastive, AP-maximization, and classification approaches, while drawing conceptual inspiration from contextual similarity work in unsupervised metric learning. citeturn10academia3turn11academia0turn41academia0turn41academia2turn12view0

A fair inference from the pose literature is that **most skeleton models are still trained for classification rather than retrieval**, and retrieval is often evaluated indirectly through one-shot recognition, zero-shot recognition, or cross-modal contrastive alignment. That is important because it means your project should not assume a well-established “standard pose retrieval benchmark” already exists in the same way that image retrieval benchmarks do. You will probably need to define the retrieval problem explicitly: query sequence versus gallery, label-based relevance, Recall@K and mAP, and robustness curves under synthetic corruption. That evaluation framing is consistent with your brief and with the general retrieval literature. fileciteturn0file0L392-L407 fileciteturn0file0L852-L860 citeturn27academia0turn38academia3turn2academia3

The noise-robustness literature around skeleton action recognition is also unusually relevant. **PoseC3D** explicitly claims robustness to pose-estimation noise. **Pose Refinement GCN** and **RA-GCN** both address incomplete or noisy skeletons directly, with RA-GCN showing reduced degradation under synthetic occlusion and jittering and PR-GCN explicitly refining poses before recognition. These are not retrieval papers, but they are useful baselines for your corruption protocol because they tell you which noise types the community already considers realistic and which robustness claims will be credible. citeturn6academia3turn37academia2turn40academia1turn40academia3

## Public baselines you can realistically build on

The **best all-around baseline for your project is MotionBERT plus a retrieval head**. The paper gives you a modern motion encoder trained from noisy partial 2D observations, and the public repository already exposes downstream workflows for 3D pose estimation, skeleton-based action recognition, and feature extraction from 2D skeleton inputs. The README documents that the pretrained encoder consumes 2D skeletons, supports up to 243 frames, assumes 17 body keypoints in H36M format, and is already adapted to action recognition through lightweight downstream heads. That makes it a strong candidate for “freeze-or-finetune encoder, replace loss with triplet/SupCon/contextual, evaluate retrieval.” citeturn1academia0turn23view2turn26view1

The **lowest-risk classical baseline is ST-GCN**. It is older, but there is public PyTorch code, the repository is widely used, and the maintainers explicitly note compatibility with MMSkeleton. The main advantage is simplicity and interpretability; the main disadvantage is that it is now historically important rather than state of the art. If your goal is a highly defensible undergraduate baseline that is easy to modify, ST-GCN is still a good option. citeturn22academia3turn23view0

The **best robustness-oriented supervised baseline is PoseC3D through MMAction2**. MMAction2 is an actively maintained toolbox that supports skeleton-based action recognition and video retrieval, and its model zoo includes ST-GCN, PoseC3D, and several stronger skeleton backbones. That matters because it saves engineering effort: you can use an existing training and checkpoint ecosystem, swap the classification loss for embedding losses, and evaluate embeddings on retrieval rather than top-1 classification. PoseC3D is especially attractive because the original paper already claims robustness to pose-estimation noise, so if contextual loss beats or stabilizes PoseC3D-derived embeddings, that is a stronger result than beating a fragile baseline. citeturn25view1turn25view0turn6academia3

If you want a **single-pose or short-sequence retrieval baseline with explicit cross-view retrieval evaluation**, **POEM / Pr-VIPE** is likely the cleanest option. The paper family is directly about view-invariant pose embeddings, retrieval across viewpoints, occlusion robustness, and later temporal embeddings for sequence retrieval. The repository is public and documents temporal support, keypoint dropout, and cross-view retrieval evaluation utilities. This is probably the best baseline if Brian wants the project to stay very close to the idea of “pose embedding” rather than “action-recognition backbone repurposed as an embedding.” citeturn2academia1turn29academia0turn24view3

If you later want a **cross-modal extension**, the public **text-to-motion-retrieval** codebase is designed to be extended with novel motion encoders, text encoders, and loss functions, and reports baseline results on HumanML3D and KIT using BiGRU, UpperLowerGRU, DG-STGCN, and a transformer motion encoder. That repository is not the right first baseline for your current project because it changes the problem from pose-to-pose retrieval to text-to-motion retrieval, but it is a very plausible second paper or extension if your contextual loss turns out to improve neighborhood structure in motion space generally. citeturn2academia3turn35view0

The **Brian contextual-loss repository itself is usable as the loss-side scaffold**. The README exposes tuned baseline settings for contrastive, triplet, multi-similarity, Proxy Anchor, Proxy NCA, and the hybrid contextual loss, and it makes explicit that the contextual setup uses structured mini-batches with multiple samples per class. For adapting to pose, that means you do not need to rediscover how the contextual loss should be mixed with standard metric losses; you mainly need to replace the image encoder with a temporal pose encoder and preserve the class-balanced batch sampler. citeturn21view0turn12view0

## Brian Kulis contextual loss and the reference map

Brian’s paper, **Supervised Metric Learning to Rank for Retrieval via Contextual Similarity Optimization**, proposes a contextual loss that optimizes **neighborhood overlap**, not only pairwise similarity. The paper states that the framework combines a contextual term with a standard contrastive term and a similarity regularizer, and it emphasizes that contextual similarity is computed at the batch level from top-k neighbors and reciprocal neighborhoods. The most operationally important design constraint is that **k is not free**: the paper sets k to the number of samples per label in the mini-batch, so the batch sampler is part of the method, not an incidental training choice. That detail transfers directly to any pose adaptation. citeturn12view0turn15view0

The code repository confirms that the public implementation is reproducible, exposes the tuned baseline hyperparameters, and includes the actual loss implementation in a small codebase rather than a giant framework. That is a major advantage for your project because it lowers adaptation cost: the contextual objective itself is already implemented, while the pose side can be swapped in through a different encoder and dataset loader. citeturn20view0turn21view0

I also pulled the **reference inventory from Brian’s paper**. The references pages show that the paper sits on four layers of prior work. The **metric-learning foundations** include Hadsell’s contrastive loss, Weinberger’s large-margin nearest neighbor formulation, Wu’s *Sampling Matters in Deep Embedding Learning*, Oh Song’s lifted structured embedding, Wang’s multi-similarity loss, and Zhai and Wu’s *Classification is a Strong Baseline for Deep Metric Learning*. The **ranking-optimized retrieval layer** includes McFee and Lanckriet’s *Metric Learning to Rank*, Cakir et al.’s *Deep Metric Learning to Rank*, Smooth-AP, ROADMAP, Revaud et al.’s average-precision training, and Rolínek et al.’s blackbox rank optimization. The **contextual and unsupervised layer** includes Zhong et al.’s k-reciprocal re-ranking, the person re-identification survey by Ye et al., and Kim et al.’s *Self-Taught Metric Learning Without Labels*. The **robustness and hierarchy layer** includes hyperbolic and hierarchical metric-learning papers such as Hyperbolic Image Embeddings, Unsupervised Hyperbolic Metric Learning, and Adaptive Hierarchical Similarity Metric Learning with Noisy Labels. The same references pages also list benchmark and application papers for CUB, Cars, SOP, DeepFashion, iNaturalist, Google Landmarks, face ID, and vehicle re-ID. citeturn17view0turn18view0

For **your** project, the most relevant Brian-paper references to read first are the following cluster: **Hadsell et al. 2006** for contrastive learning; **Weinberger et al. 2005** for nearest-neighbor metric learning; **Wu et al. 2017** for sampling and batch construction; **Zhai and Wu 2018** for strong classification baselines; **Musgrave et al. 2020** for baseline realism; **Smooth-AP 2020** for listwise ranking objectives; and **Self-Taught Metric Learning Without Labels 2022** for contextual similarity as supervision before Brian’s supervised extension. Those are the papers that tell you what contextual loss is replacing or extending. citeturn17view0turn18view0turn10academia3turn41academia0turn41academia1turn41academia2

## Recommended experimental design

The most defensible first-stage project is this: **NTU RGB+D 120 skeleton retrieval, sequence encoder baseline, retrieval evaluation under synthetic pose corruption, then contextual-loss adaptation**. That exact path already matches the direction in your brief, which recommends NTU RGB+D 120 first, controlled noise injection, and later optional additions such as SportsPose or AMASS. Start with sequence-level embeddings and gallery retrieval by action label under cross-subject and cross-view splits; report Recall@1, Recall@5, Recall@10, and mAP; then measure degradation under joint jitter, keypoint dropout, frame dropout, viewpoint perturbation, and left-right swaps. fileciteturn0file0L547-L553 fileciteturn0file0L837-L848 fileciteturn0file0L889-L899 citeturn6academia0turn40academia1turn6academia3

For the **baseline stack**, I would recommend three levels. First, a **classification baseline with embedding extraction** from ST-GCN, PoseC3D, or MotionBERT, because classification-trained embeddings are still a strong reality-check baseline in metric learning. Second, a **standard metric-learning baseline** such as triplet loss or supervised contrastive loss on the same backbone. Third, a **contextual-loss version** using Brian’s batch construction and hybrid objective. This design is scientifically cleaner than comparing contextual loss only against cross-entropy, because the real question is whether contextual neighborhoods beat standard pairwise or batch-contrastive embedding training. citeturn41academia0turn10academia3turn11academia0turn12view0turn21view0

If you want the **highest probability of a working baseline with manageable engineering**, I would use **MotionBERT** or **PoseC3D**. MotionBERT gives you a pretrained motion representation already exposed as an encoder over 2D skeleton sequences and already adapted to action recognition; PoseC3D gives you a supervised skeleton representation that explicitly advertises robustness to pose-estimation noise and is available in MMAction2. If compute and code complexity are the top constraints, ST-GCN is the easiest starting point. If conceptual closeness to “pose embedding” is the top priority, use POEM or Pr-VIPE as a side baseline or a separate smaller experiment. citeturn26view1turn25view1turn6academia3turn23view0turn24view3

The most compelling **paper claim** you could realistically support is not “contextual loss wins best clean retrieval on every benchmark.” A stronger and more believable claim is narrower: **contextual metric learning preserves retrieval neighborhood quality better than standard embedding losses as pose noise increases**. That is also the point where your project becomes more than a simple application paper. Brian’s method was motivated by semantic inconsistency and label brittleness in image retrieval; your adaptation would test whether neighborhood-aware supervision is also beneficial when the problem is **input corruption in pose space** rather than only label noise in image space. citeturn12view0turn15view0 fileciteturn0file0L426-L427 fileciteturn0file0L852-L860
